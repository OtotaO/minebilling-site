Timely filing deadline calculator

How long do you have to file the claim? Pick the payer, enter the date of service, and see the filing deadline, the days remaining, and where that number comes from. Every limit here is quoted from the payer's own published policy or the governing regulation, with its source and as-of date shown next to the answer. Everything runs in your browser — no patient data, no sign-up, nothing leaves your device.

Whether your contract sets the deadline depends on the payer — so pick one first

For commercial payers, your contract is the deadline, not this page. Published national numbers from UnitedHealthcare, Cigna, Aetna and BCBSIL are defaults or floors. The executed participation agreement controls, and it can be shorter. A self-funded employer group can set a different deadline for one member than for the next, and state law can override both. UnitedHealthcare says it plainly: “Timely filing limits vary based on state requirements and contracts. Refer to your Agreement for your specific timely filing requirements.” So the field below marked my contract or program says ___ days is the one to use for any commercial payer whose agreement you have in hand — it overrides the published default.

For Medicare and Medicaid the reverse holds. Those limits are regulatory or statutory — 42 CFR 424.44(a) for Medicare, 42 CFR 447.45(d)(1) for the federal Medicaid ceiling, state law for the state programs — and no participation agreement lengthens them. If you type a longer number against one of those payers, the calculator keeps the published limit and tells you it did.

The same applies to a limit the payer publishes as a floor it is required to allow. One row here is of that kind: UnitedHealthcare Medicare Advantage, non-contracted providers, where UHC's own wording is “we are required to allow 365 days from the through date of service for noncontracted health care providers to submit claims for processing.” A floor is not a negotiated term, so a typed number cannot push it out either.

Because of that split, the payer field is required. A number of days with no payer selected cannot be judged — we would not know whether it is allowed to apply — so the calculator will not produce a date until you choose one. If your payer is not in the list, choose one of the two Not listed options at the bottom of the picker to say which kind it is; the calculator will then use your number and say plainly that it is unverified.

How this calculator counts

Three things are easy to get wrong, so they are worth stating:

  • A calendar-year rule is not “365 days.” Medicare's limit is one calendar year from the date of service, so a 1 March 2023 service is timely through 1 March 2024 — 366 days, because 2024 is a leap year. Counting 365 days would put the deadline a day early. CMS also states the leap-day case explicitly: “Claims having a date of service on February 29 must be filed by February 28 of the following year to be considered timely filed.” This page applies calendar arithmetic where the rule is expressed in years or months, and day arithmetic only where the payer publishes a day count.
  • Day-count rules are computed as start date + N calendar days. If your payer counts the date of service itself as day 1, the true deadline is one day earlier than shown. Payers rarely publish which convention they use, so treat the displayed date as the latest possible deadline and never file on it.
  • The clock runs to receipt, not to submission. Medicare measures against the contractor's receipt date; Illinois HFS stamps a Document Control Number encoding the Julian date it received the claim; UnitedHealthcare measures to “the date by which UnitedHealthcare, or its delegate, receives the claim.” Clearinghouse transit and rework time comes out of your margin, not theirs.

The “closing soon / act now” bands (30 and 14 days) are our own working thresholds for triage, not payer rules. No payer publishes them.

What this calculator does not know

These are deliberately absent. An honest gap costs you a phone call; an invented deadline costs you the claim.

  • Aetna's national commercial limit. Aetna's current national provider manual for health care professionals contains no timely-filing day count — we searched the full text. Widely repeated third-party figures (90 days, 180 days, 12 months) could not be traced to any Aetna-published document, so none is recorded here. Read your agreement or call Aetna provider services, then use the contract field. The New York row above exists only because New York law imposes it, and it does not generalise to Illinois or anywhere else.
  • UnitedHealthcare commercial, participating. UHC publishes only a floor — “we, or our capitated care provider, allow at least 90 days for participating health care providers” — and then tells you the Agreement sets the actual number. There is no national participating figure to calculate with. Do not borrow the 180-day non-participating row for an in-network clinic.
  • Newly-enrolled Medicare suppliers billing for services before their billing privileges took effect. In this one case the calculator is more generous than the regulation: it applies the ordinary one-year period and knows nothing about your enrollment date. 42 CFR 424.44(a)(1) opens “Except as provided in paragraphs (b) and (e) of this section,” and paragraph (e) reads in full: “As specified in §§ 424.520 and 424.521 of this subpart, there are restrictions on the ability of the following newly-enrolled suppliers to submit claims for items or services furnished prior to the effective date of their Medicare billing privileges: (1) Physician or nonphysician practitioner organizations. (2) Physicians. (3) Nonphysician practitioners. (4) Independent diagnostic testing facilities.” The two cross-referenced sections are where the actual restriction lives: 42 CFR 424.520(d)(1) sets the effective date of billing privileges for physicians, non-physician practitioners and their organizations (among others) as “the later of— (i) The date of filing of a Medicare enrollment application that was subsequently approved by a Medicare contractor; or (ii) The date that the provider or supplier first began furnishing services at a new practice location,” and 42 CFR 424.521(a)(1) lets those same suppliers “retrospectively bill for services when the provider or supplier has met all program requirements (including State licensure requirements), and services were provided at the enrolled practice location for up to— (i) Thirty days prior to their effective date if circumstances precluded enrollment in advance of providing services to Medicare beneficiaries; or (ii) Ninety days prior to their effective date if a Presidentially-declared disaster under the Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act, 42 U.S.C. 5121-5206 (Stafford Act) precluded enrollment in advance of providing services to Medicare beneficiaries.” So for a new physician, NP or PA — or a practice at a new location — the binding date can be the retrospective billing window, not the one-year filing period, and it can be far shorter. We have not built that in: it depends on your enrollment application date, your approval, and which of the two windows applies. Quoted from the current eCFR text of 42 CFR 424.44, 424.520 and 424.521, retrieved 2026-08-03 (title 42 as issued 2026-07-30); paragraph (e) is identical in the 2023 annual edition linked below.
  • Illinois Medicaid managed care. The 180-day HFS number above is fee-for-service only. HealthChoice Illinois MCOs set their own deadlines by contract, and a large share of Illinois Medicaid volume is managed care. We did not source those.
  • Corrected claims usually get no new clock. Medicare: an adjustment to add an item or service is “not permitted after the expiration of the time limitation for filing of the initial claim.” UnitedHealthcare: the original day count “also applies to submission of corrected claims.” BCBSIL: “Corrected claims must still be filed within the timely filing deadlines” — and you cannot correct until the original has adjudicated. New York Medicaid is the one exception we found, with its own 60-day-from-notification window. Calculate corrections from the original date of service.
  • Appeal and reconsideration deadlines. Separate from filing limits and frequently shorter. Not researched here, and they must not be inferred from the numbers above.
  • Humana, Elevance/Anthem, Centene/Ambetter, Molina, TRICARE, and every Blue plan outside Illinois. Not sourced in this pass, so they are omitted rather than approximated.
  • Medicare reopenings (Pub 100-04 Chapter 34) and any general rule that Medicare as secondary payer gets extra time. The only secondary-claim extension we could verify is the narrow one for claims filed after a bankrupt primary payer's liquidation. Absent a verified exception, plan on the ordinary one-year limit.

Common questions

What is the Medicare timely filing limit?

One calendar year from the date of service. 42 CFR 424.44(a)(1): “Except as provided in paragraphs (b) and (e) of this section, for services furnished on or after January 1, 2010, the claim must be filed no later than the close of the period ending 1 calendar year after the date of service.” For professional claims the clock starts on the line-item “From” date; for institutional span-date claims it starts on the “Through” date. Note what CMS says about missing it: a denial for untimely filing “does not constitute an initial determination. As such, the determination that a claim was not filed timely is not subject to appeal.” Limited regulatory exceptions exist at 42 CFR 424.44(b), and there are four of them: administrative error; retroactive Medicare entitlement; retroactive Medicare entitlement where a State Medicaid agency recovered its payment 6 months or more after the service; and retroactive disenrollment from a Medicare Advantage plan or a PACE organization. There is no general good-cause escape. One extension is automatic rather than discretionary — 42 CFR 424.44(c): if the last day of the period falls on a “Federal nonworkday (a Saturday, Sunday, legal holiday, or a day which by statute or Executive Order is declared to be a nonworkday for Federal employees), the time is extended to the next succeeding workday.” This calculator does not apply that extension, so the date it shows is the earlier, safer one. Note the other exception named in paragraph (a)(1): paragraph (e) restricts claims from newly-enrolled suppliers for services furnished before their Medicare billing privileges took effect, and it runs the other way — it can make the real deadline much shorter than a year. See what this calculator does not know.

How long do I have to file a claim?

It depends entirely on who you are billing, and for commercial payers on what you signed. The range in the table above runs from 90 days (New York Medicaid, Cigna participating) to a full calendar year (Medicare). Two claims to the same commercial payer, from the same clinic, in the same week can carry different deadlines because the two members work for different self-funded employers.

Does a clearinghouse rejection count as a filed claim?

No — and this is one of the most expensive silent failures in a small practice. UnitedHealthcare states it directly: “UnitedHealthcare claims that are rejected are not proof of timely filing as rejections require further action from the provider before the claim can be adjudicated.” A claim sitting in a rejection queue is, for deadline purposes, a claim that was never filed — and it will not appear on an aging report that only counts claims you believe you submitted.

Which date starts the clock on a secondary claim?

Not the date of service, and not the same event for every payer. Cigna measures from “the processing date indicated on the primary carrier's Explanation of Benefits (EOB) or Explanation of Payment (EOP).” BCBSIL measures from “the date of the participating provider's receipt of the explanation of benefits from the primary payer”. UnitedHealthcare measures from “the day of payment, contest, denial or notice from the primary payer.” Processing date and receipt date are not the same day. Read the exact trigger before you count.

Why do these numbers go stale?

Payers republish without notice. The Texas Medicaid manual is reissued monthly; the Cigna policy page behind the 90/180-day figures carries a page date of 2015-04-01; the Illinois HFS document was last revised 2022-12-12. That is why every row here shows its as-of date instead of hiding it. Re-verify before a deadline decision.

How many claims are already past the deadline?

Most practices do not know, because a claim that was rejected upstream never shows up as unpaid.
